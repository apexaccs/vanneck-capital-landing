Liquidation preference explained with one worked example

A founder gets a term sheet for a $500K investment at a $10M post-money valuation. The valuation line is clear. The line about liquidation preference is one sentence long, and it decides who is paid first if the company is sold. This article explains that sentence with one worked example.

What a liquidation preference is

A liquidation preference is the amount an investor is paid first when a company is sold or wound up, before common shareholders receive anything. Common shareholders are usually the founders and the team. The preference is written as a multiple of the investment. A 1x preference means the investor can take back one times its money.

Participating and non-participating

A non-participating preference means the investor chooses one of two options: take its preference, or convert to shares and take its percentage of the proceeds. It does not receive both. A participating preference pays the preference first and then also a share of what is left. Founders receive less under a participating preference in the same sale.

A worked example

Vanneck Capital invests $500K at a $10M post-money valuation. Ownership is 5% ($500K / $10M). The preference is 1x, non-participating. Here is what the investor receives in three sales.

  • Sale at $8M. 5% is $400K ($8M x 5%). That is less than $500K, so the investor takes the preference. Everyone else shares $7.5M ($8M - $500K).
  • Sale at $10M. 5% is $500K ($10M x 5%). Both options pay the same.
  • Sale at $20M. 5% is $1M ($20M x 5%). That is more than $500K, so the investor converts and receives $1M. Everyone else shares $19M.

The preference only matters when the sale price is below the post-money valuation. Above it, the investor converts and the preference is not used.

What the example leaves out

The example assumes one investor and one class of shares. Real companies have earlier rounds, which may rank ahead or alongside. It ignores debt, legal fees, unvested options and the option pool. It also ignores taxes and any escrow in the sale. Each of these changes the numbers.

What to check in a term sheet

  1. The multiple. 1x is standard. Anything higher needs a reason.
  2. Participating or not. Ask for the answer in writing.
  3. Seniority. Ask where the preference ranks against other investors.
  4. Caps. If a preference is participating, ask whether the total is capped.
  5. Conversion. Ask whether conversion to common shares is automatic or optional.

Our standard terms at Vanneck are a 1x non-participating liquidation preference.
