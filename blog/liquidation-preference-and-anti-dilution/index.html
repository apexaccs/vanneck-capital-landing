Liquidation preference and anti-dilution, in plain terms

Most term sheet arguments are about valuation. Two other clauses often matter more when a company is sold or raises at a lower price. These are the versions in Vanneck's standard terms, with numbers so you can check them yourself.

Liquidation preference: 1x, non-participating

A liquidation preference decides who is paid first when the company is sold. "1x" means the investor can take back the amount it invested before common shareholders receive anything. "Non-participating" means the investor must choose: take that money back, or convert to shares and share in the proceeds. It cannot do both.

Take a $500K investment at a $10M post-money valuation. That is 5% of the company.

  • The company is sold for $6M. Five percent is $300K, which is less than $500K, so the investor takes its $500K back. The founders and others share the remaining $5.5M.
  • The company is sold for $20M. Five percent is $1M, which is more than $500K, so the investor converts and receives $1M. The other $19M goes to everyone else.

The investor receives whichever amount is larger, never both. That is the difference from a participating preference, where the investor would take its money back and then also share in the rest.

Anti-dilution: broad-based weighted average

Anti-dilution protects an investor if the company later sells shares at a lower price than the investor paid. Broad-based weighted average is the milder form. It adjusts the investor's conversion price only partly, and it counts all existing shares and options in the calculation, so a small down round causes a small adjustment.

The formula is:

New price = Old price x (A + B) / (A + C)

  • A is the number of shares outstanding before the new round, including options.
  • B is the number of shares the new money would have bought at the old price.
  • C is the number of shares actually issued in the new round.

An example. The investor paid $1.00 per share. There are 10,000,000 shares before the new round. The company then raises $1M at $0.50 per share.

  • A is 10,000,000.
  • C is 2,000,000, because $1M at $0.50 buys that many shares.
  • B is 1,000,000, because $1M at the old price of $1.00 would have bought that many.
  • New price is $1.00 x 11,000,000 / 12,000,000, which is about $0.92.

The investor's price falls from $1.00 to about $0.92, not all the way to $0.50. A harsher clause, full ratchet, would have moved it to $0.50 and shifted much more of the cost to the founders.

What to check in any term sheet

  1. Is the preference 1x, and is it non-participating? A higher multiple or participation changes the exit maths a lot.
  2. Is anti-dilution broad-based weighted average, or full ratchet?
  3. Does a down round trigger any other rights beyond the price adjustment?
  4. Run both examples above with your own numbers before you sign.

If a term sheet departs from these standard terms, ask why.
